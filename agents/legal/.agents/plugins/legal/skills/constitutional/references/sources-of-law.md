# Sources of American law (orientation map)

Distilled from "Overview of the American Legal System," University of
Virginia School of Law (YouTube, `d75upaDHSvY`, published 2018-08-23,
~39 min; transcript pulled 2026-09-11). An orientation-level map of the
system — useful for framing, not itself citable authority; every
proposition below still requires verification against primary sources
under the `general` skill's methods.

## Concurrent sovereignty

Anywhere in the United States, a person is subject to more than one
system of law at once: federal law and the law of the state they are
in. Both systems run in parallel and each has the same four-layer
internal structure — constitution, statutes, regulations, common law.

Cities and counties are not a third sovereign. Local governments are
units of the state: their power exists only by delegation from state
law, and a local ordinance ranks below everything in the state system
that delegated it. Even the largest city governments (e.g. New York
City) derive their authority from their state.

## The four sources, by branch

| Source       | Made by                                                                         | Nature                                                                                             |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Constitution | Framing/ratification and Article V amendment                                    | Defines the government's structure and powers; source of everything below                          |
| Statutes     | Legislature                                                                     | Positive enactments under constitutionally granted power                                           |
| Regulations  | Executive agencies (and occasionally other bodies) under legislative delegation | The delegate elaborates a statute's scheme (e.g. EPA regulations fleshing out the Clean Water Act) |
| Common law   | Courts                                                                          | Judge-made law with no enacted text behind it; the rule _is_ the accumulated case law              |

Two structural observations worth keeping:

- **All three branches are sources of law.** The executive is not only
  an enforcer: through delegation it defines binding rules. Delegation
  need not run to an executive agency — under the Rules Enabling Act,
  Congress delegated the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure to the
  Supreme Court (advised by its rules committees), so a body of
  governing law is made by the judiciary under statutory delegation.
- **The judiciary is doubly placed**: it is the source of the common
  law _and_ the interpreter of every other source — constitutions,
  statutes, and regulations alike.

## Common law specifics

- Common law predates the founding: the states carried their colonial,
  English-derived common law into the constitutional era. This makes
  the U.S. (like England) a common-law system, in contrast to the
  civil-law codes of continental Europe that attempt to define every
  rule by positive enactment.
- Within each system, an on-point statute or valid regulation
  **displaces** the common law; the common law governs only where no
  enacted law occupies the question.
- **Federal common law is limited.** Its scope was sharply restricted
  in the early twentieth century (the _Erie_ line, treated in the
  `civil-procedure` skill), but enclaves persist — admiralty is the
  classic example. Never assume a general federal common law of torts
  or contracts exists.
- Whole subjects map onto this displacement story: torts and property
  remain largely state common law; contracts straddles common law and
  code (the UCC, adopted state by state, governs contracts for goods);
  criminal law has moved uniformly from common law to statute (with
  the Model Penal Code influencing, though rarely wholly enacted in,
  state codes) — partly because enacted codes give the notice of
  criminality that legality demands.
- First-year "general" common law is a teaching abstraction of
  principles most states share; an actual matter always requires the
  specific jurisdiction's law.

## Jurisdiction versus supremacy

Keep two questions separate:

1. **Which law governs** — the supremacy/preemption analysis in
   `SKILL.md`.
2. **Which court may decide** — jurisdiction. The system rejects both
   easy answers (complete separation; everyone decides everything):
   state courts decide state _and_ federal questions, and federal
   courts decide federal _and_ state questions, each only under
   conditions that are themselves contested law. A state court
   applying federal law is bound by the Supremacy Clause's command
   that "the judges in every state shall be bound thereby."

On supremacy itself, the lecture's framing matches the skill's:
Article VI, Clause 2 makes the Constitution and laws made "in
pursuance thereof" supreme, but the operative work is deciding whether
an actual conflict exists. Federalism as a value counsels courts to
read federal and state law into peaceful coexistence where possible —
the preemption inquiry — and judicial review itself is not spelled out
in the constitutional text; it was confirmed by the early Supreme
Court (_Marbury v. Madison_).
