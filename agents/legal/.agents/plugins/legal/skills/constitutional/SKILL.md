# Supremacy

Analyze which law controls an issue and whether an exercise of government
power survives constitutional limits. The Constitution is the supreme law of
the land under Article VI, Clause 2; every other authority ranks beneath it
and applies only within the power that created it. Treat supremacy as a
question to be analyzed with the record and inspected authority, never as a
slogan that decides the case by itself.

Frame the work in the profession's structure — IRAC/CREAC: state the issue as
a neutral question, synthesize the governing rule from inspected authority,
apply it to the sourced facts, address the strongest contrary analysis, and
conclude with calibrated confidence. Use `jurisprudence` for reasoning
discipline and `general` for research, citation and verification methods.

## Hierarchy of authority

Order the candidate authorities before weighing any of them:

1. The U.S. Constitution, as authoritatively construed by the Supreme Court
   (judicial review per _Marbury v. Madison_ — a role confirmed by the Court,
   not spelled out in the text; binding effect per _Cooper v. Aaron_).
2. Federal statutes and ratified treaties made pursuant to the Constitution.
   An unconstitutional statute controls nothing.
3. Valid federal regulations and executive action — only within authority
   actually delegated by statute or the Constitution, and only if adopted
   through required procedure. Delegation may run to bodies other than
   executive agencies (e.g. the Rules Enabling Act delegates the Federal
   Rules of Civil Procedure to the Supreme Court).
4. Federal common law — judge-made law with no enacted text behind it.
   Limited to recognized enclaves (admiralty is the classic example) after
   the _Erie_ line; never assume a general federal common law exists, and
   treat it as displaced by any on-point federal statute or valid regulation.
5. State constitutions, then state statutes, then state regulations, then
   state common law, then local law, each subject to every level above it —
   and a state constitution may grant _more_ individual protection than the
   federal floor, never less. Local governments are units of the state:
   their power exists only by delegation from state law, so an ordinance
   ranks below the state authority that authorized it.

Within each sovereign's system, an on-point statute or valid regulation
displaces the common law; the common law governs only where no enacted law
occupies the question. And the two systems run concurrently — any person or
transaction in the United States is subject to federal law and at least one
state's law at once, each with this same internal structure (constitution,
statutes, regulations, common law).

Rank does not answer applicability. A superior authority displaces an
inferior one only where both actually govern the same question for this
jurisdiction, party and period. Confirm each authority's jurisdiction,
effective dates and current treatment before relying on its rank. See
`references/sources-of-law.md` for an orientation map of the sources, their
branch of origin, and the common-law/code distinction.

## Analytical sequence

Apply the steps that the issue requires, in order, and record which were
reached:

1. **Threshold.** Identify the forum and posture, and whether the question is
   justiciable: standing, ripeness, mootness, political question. Determine
   whether state action is present when an individual-rights claim is raised.
   Keep which-law-governs distinct from which-court-decides: jurisdiction is
   concurrent, not separated — state courts decide state and federal
   questions, and federal courts decide federal and state questions, each
   only under conditions that are themselves contested law (see the
   `civil-procedure` skill). A state court applying federal law is bound by
   the Supremacy Clause's command to "the judges in every state."
2. **Source of power.** Locate the affirmative power for the government act:
   an enumerated federal power (with Necessary and Proper support) for
   federal action, or the general police power for state action. Federal
   action without an enumerated source fails before any rights analysis.
3. **Structural limits.** Test separation of powers, federalism limits
   (anti-commandeering, state sovereign immunity), and any
   jurisdiction-specific structural constraint the record raises.
4. **Preemption.** Where federal and state law overlap, analyze express
   preemption from statutory text, field preemption from a pervasive federal
   scheme, and conflict preemption — impossibility of dual compliance or
   obstacle to federal purposes. The operative question is whether an actual
   conflict exists: federalism counsels reading federal and state law into
   peaceful coexistence where possible, so apply the presumption against
   preemption in fields of traditional state regulation, and note where its
   current force is contested. Supremacy resolves conflicts; it does not
   manufacture them.
5. **Individual rights and scrutiny.** Identify the right burdened and the
   governing framework: rational basis, intermediate scrutiny, strict
   scrutiny, or a doctrine-specific test (history-and-tradition, undue
   burden, forum analysis). State who bears the burden and what tailoring
   the tier demands; do not import a tier from a different doctrine.
6. **Remedy and severability.** State what invalidity would mean here:
   facial versus as-applied challenge, severability of the offending
   provision, and the relief the posture actually permits.

## Interpretive modalities

Construct and evaluate constitutional arguments through the recognized
professional modalities: text, structure, history and original meaning,
precedent, prudential consequences, and national ethos. Identify which
modality each argument uses and represent competing modalities in their
strongest supported form; disagreement between modalities is a finding to
report, not a defect to hide. Apply the avoidance canon — prefer a plausible
construction that avoids the constitutional question — and say when it, not
the merits, drives the conclusion.
